The Developer Perspective

Everyone has to make money, and when it comes to gachas, which are often full of sprawling storylines, tons of voice acting, and years-long storylines, that takes a lot of money to produce. These games cannot be made for free, so how should they be monetized?

Typically, these games would come at a fairly high upfront cost, but that also conflicts with the consistently updating storyline many of them have. On release, a game would likely have a wildly different value than 3 years in, when 100+ hours of playtime have been added.

Another method of monetization would be ads, but that would likely decrease the perceived quality of the game. No one likes ads, especially in a game that relies heavily on story not being interrupted by an unskippable minute of unrelated content. Many gacha games also require “grinding,” the process of spending lots of time doing a repetitive task in game to gather materials. Ads would take this from a background process to a constantly interrupted annoyance.

For ambitious developers, the gacha model works. They allow for a wide range of players (who are more willing to download the original game with no monetary commitment), along with a fairly reliable source of income that can scale with the ever-increasing cast of characters and amount of players the servers must support.

However, devs also need to be careful with the details of their gacha. A gacha too easy won’t bring in enough money, but a gacha with rates too low will not only alienate the F2P playerbase, but earn the game a reputation as a cashgrab amongst Whales. Even the most generous players don’t like feeling exploited. Devs need to find a balance between generosity and risk: that’s where the pity system comes into play.
