The Parent Perspective

So your kid showed you something called a “gacha game.” You’re not sure what it is, but it looks risky.

Well, it can be, but that doesn’t automatically mean it’s unsafe for your child to play.

The first thing to consider is the age and maturity of your child, as well as what game they’re interested in. Gacha games have a wide range of target audiences. Games like the Infinity Nikki and Umamusume are lighthearted and directed more towards tween girls, while games like Reverse: 1999 and most Hoyoverse games are more designed for older teens and young adults, with darker plotlines and more mature scenes. Then there’s the gacha games that are strictly adult only. If a child wants to play a game, it’s the parents responsibility to ensure the content is appropriate, and this is even more important when a gacha is involved. The “severity” or fairness of the gacha is often also correlated with age range, though not always: high rarity cards aren’t really needed for Umamusume, but you might find it difficult to progress in Genshin Impact without a high rarity team.

Young children and teenagers lack the forethought and impulse control to prevent reckless spending. They may be banned from casinos, but gachas for collectible characters and outfits are designed to draw children in. That leaves parents responsible for teaching their children the dangers of gacha use, especially if the child is using their money.

The most important thing here is communication: a study conducted by Gabriel A. Brooks and Luke Clark in 2019 concluded that the biggest risk factor for risky gacha-gambling behavior was false beliefs about gambling. Before starting a gacha game, sit your child down and set some boundaries. Make sure they understand that gacha is gambling, and ensure they understand and accept the risk of bad luck and not getting the outcome they want. If they’re an older child with their own money, work with them to plan a spending limit. Gacha games thrive on gambling addicts who don’t realize they’re gambling.

Whether or not to let children play gacha games is a personal decision, but they are growing increasingly popular, and if your child is a gamer, it’s likely they’ll pick one up at some point. Finding safer games and clearly communicating risks can go a long way.
